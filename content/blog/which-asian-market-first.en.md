A brand going global should enter first the Asian market where four things line up: proven demand for its category, a price that still works after import costs, channels it can reach with confidence, and a team that can operate in that market’s time zone and language. Market size matters less than fit. Choosing the wrong order means strong execution only amplifies the mistake faster.

## Why does the order of market entry matter?

The order of market entry matters because the first market shapes the budget, team and learning for every market after it. A first market that fits produces proof points, local content and creator relationships that make the second market cheaper. A first market that does not fit burns budget and confidence before the brand has learned anything it can reuse.

Two lessons follow from the table. First, the largest markets are not automatically the easiest: Indonesia has more than ten times as many internet users as Taiwan, but a launch there needs far more budget and local capability. Second, each market runs on a different platform mix, so a channel plan built for one market rarely transfers unchanged to the next.

## What criteria should decide the first market?

Five criteria should decide the first market, weighted by your product and your resources:

1. **Category demand:** Is there proven demand, and which local or international brands already serve it?
2. **Price fit:** Does the price still compete once import duties, logistics and local margins are included?
3. **Channel access:** Can you reach buyers on the platforms they actually use, such as LINE in Taiwan or WhatsApp in Malaysia?
4. **Regulatory effort:** How much work do labelling, registration or category-specific rules require before you can sell?
5. **Operating capacity:** Do you have people who can make decisions in the market’s time zone and language?

A market that scores well on all five is usually a better first choice than a larger market that scores well on only one or two.

## When should a brand hold off on a market?

A brand should hold off on a market when the warning signs outweigh the opportunity. Common red flags include:

- **No clear buyer:** you cannot name who buys the product in that market and why they would switch.
- **Price that only works at scale:** margins disappear unless volumes are far above what a first year can deliver.
- **Regulatory work that exceeds the budget:** registration or labelling costs would consume most of the launch budget.
- **No one to run it:** decisions would wait days for a reply from another time zone.

Holding off is not a failure. It keeps budget for the market where the brand can win first, and the market can be revisited once the brand has proof points.

## How do you sequence the next markets?

Sequence the next markets by reusing what the first market proved. After a successful first launch, look for markets that share the most with it, such as language, buyer profile or platform habits, and plan to rebuild what does not carry over. For example, Taiwan and Hong Kong both use Traditional Chinese, but their messaging apps differ sharply: GWI’s Q2 2026 data shows 92.0% monthly LINE use in Taiwan, while Hong Kong’s top messaging app is WhatsApp at 69.2%.

## FAQ

### Should a brand enter the biggest Asian market first?

Not necessarily. The biggest markets usually need more budget, more local capability and longer timelines. The best first market is the one where demand, price, channels and operating capacity line up, which is often a smaller, highly connected market.

### How many markets should a brand enter at once?

Most brands should start with one market and add the next once the first has produced proof points. Entering several markets at once spreads budget and attention thinly and makes it harder to learn what works.

### How long does it take to know if a market is working?

Eastbright Global typically sees advertising and conversion metrics start to move within one to two months, while word-of-mouth and organic traffic generally take three to six months to show a clear upward trend.
